# How many clinics could buy the assistant

Written for: the owner. 25 November 2026. Three research agents
gathered the figures; the arithmetic and the conclusions are mine and
are marked where they are estimates.

## The answer in five lines

- **Could buy, in theory:** about 6 to 7 lakh private allopathic doctors
  in India, in something like 6 lakh clinics. The clinic count is the
  weakest number in this report.
- **Already pay for clinic software:** roughly 60,000 to 1,20,000
  doctors in all of India. That is the real market today, not the
  6 lakh.
- **In your languages:** Maharashtra plus the Hindi belt hold about
  39 percent of India's doctors, so roughly 23,000 to 47,000 doctors who
  pay for software. Maharashtra alone: about 9,000 to 18,000.
- **What you alone can win:** by my model, about 30 paying clinics after
  one year and 55 after three, with a range of 6 to 74 and 10 to 160.
  Churn, not market size, is what caps it.
- **What that earns:** in the middle case about ₹9 lakh a year by year
  three. The high case is about ₹38 lakh.

Every large company in this market got there with investors' money, a
free tier, or pharma companies paying. None did it as one person selling
subscriptions.

Most private hospitals are small. One source puts about 45,000 of
India's 80,000 hospitals under 30 beds; that source is a blog with no
dataset named.

### Where they are

- About 73 percent of doctors work in cities.
- Maharashtra has 15.1 percent of India's registered allopathic doctors,
  the most of any state. The ten Hindi-belt states together have 23.9
  percent.
- So a Marathi, Hindi and English product speaks to about 39 percent of
  India's doctors.

## 2. How many already pay for software

Nobody publishes how many private clinics use software. These are the
pieces:

| Evidence | Figure | How solid |
|---|---|---|
| Hospitals using electronic records | about 35 percent, up from 18 percent in 2018 | Bain, September 2026; hospitals only, mostly large chains |
| Private facilities on government-linked (ABDM) software | 21,479 | Official, December 2024 |
| Eka Care | 65,000 doctors, **about 12,000 paying** | The only disclosed paying count in the market; company's own |
| Practo Ray | "50,000+ doctors", ₹999 to ₹1,499 a month | Company claim; not audited |
| HealthPlix | 14,000+ doctors; earns from pharma as well as subscriptions | Company claim |
| EkaScribe (the AI note-writer) | 1,000+ doctors, a year after launch | Company claim |
| Small clinics in a Mumbai pilot | "often don't use digital clinic management software" | An NGO's field report, 2022 |

What the leaders earn tells the same story. Eka Care took in about
₹26 crore in its last reported year, HealthPlix about ₹38 crore, both at
a loss. Dividing Eka's revenue by its paying doctors gives about ₹22,000
a doctor a year, which matches its list price.

**My estimate:** 60,000 to 1,20,000 Indian doctors pay for clinic
software today, about one in ten of those in private practice. The top
of that range rests on Practo's own claim.

**For AI note-writing specifically:** very early. The one number is
Eka's 1,000 doctors.

About ₹1,000 to ₹2,000 a month is the most a single-doctor clinic has
been shown to pay. No survey of what Indian doctors are willing to pay
was found.

## 4. How many you could win

No one publishes how well software sells to Indian clinics: not the
share of demos that close, not what a salesperson closes in a month, not
how long a sale takes. So this model uses the nearest figures there are,
and three of its four inputs are assumptions.

| Input | Low | Middle | High | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Demos you give a month | 15 | 25 | 40 | My assumption for one founder; the high case needs a second person |
| Share of demos that become paying | 5% | 13% | 18% | Not measured for clinics. 13% and 18% are free-to-paid rates at Vyapar and Eka Care |
| Share of customers lost each month | 7% | 5% | 3% | General small-business software ranges, not clinics |
| Price for a clinic a month | ₹600 | ₹1,350 | ₹2,000 | Section 3 |

| | Low | Middle | High |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paying clinics after 1 year | 6 | 30 | 74 |
| Paying clinics after 3 years | 10 | 55 | 160 |
| Where it levels off | 11 | 65 | 240 |
| Yearly income at year 3 | ₹72,000 | ₹8.9 lakh | ₹38 lakh |

Two things to take from this:

- **The market is not the limit.** Even the high case is under one
  percent of Maharashtra's clinics. The limits are how many demos you
  can give and how many customers stay.
- **Churn sets the ceiling.** At 5 percent lost a month you level off at
  65 clinics however long you sell. Halving churn doubles the business;
  doubling demos also does, but costs a salary.

For comparison, Practo took about three years and a team of eight to
reach 10,000 doctors "onboarded", and HealthPlix aimed for 450 doctors
in its first year. Both had investors.

## 5. What works against you

- **Free alternatives are arriving.** Eka Care has a free tier. The
  National Health Authority chose Eka in November 2026 for a voice
  scribe for public facilities in 15 or more languages. The government
  also launched free software for small clinics in 2026, with 2,200
  facilities on it.
- **Hindi and Marathi voice is not unique.** Eka and Augnito both claim
  it. No product was found selling a Marathi voice assistant
  specifically, but that is an absence in a search, not proof of a gap.
- **The speech engine may be the wrong one.** A December 2025 study of
  162 recordings in a busy Indian hospital measured word errors in Hindi
  of 39 percent for Sarvam, which we use, against 18.5 percent for
  Gemini 2.5 Pro. It is a preprint from a psychiatry department with no
  Marathi, so it is a warning and not a verdict.
- **Time saved is smaller than sellers say.** The one randomised trial
  found a 9.5 percent cut in note-writing time for one product and none
  for another. In a US study only 15 percent of AI notes were saved
  without edits. Indian consultations are already two to ten minutes.
- **The doctor stays liable.** India has no rule that shifts
  responsibility to the software.
- **Consent rules bite in May 2027.** Recording a consultation will need
  consent that is specific and informed.

## 6. What works for you

- **The other half is unsold.** Every product found sells note-writing.
  None found sells an assistant that registers the patient, takes the
  payment and enters the lab result by voice, inside each person's
  permissions.
- **You are in the largest state market.** Maharashtra has more doctors
  than any other state, and its clinics are about to be registered for
  the first time.
- **Your costs are low.** Commands cost under ₹150 a month for a whole
  clinic to serve. Listening is the part that needs metering.
- **You already have software to attach it to.** Rivals sell a scribe
  that must be fitted to someone else's records. Yours is built in.

## 7. What I would do with this

1. **Sell to clinics that already pay for software, not to those that
   use none.** That is one in ten, and they have shown they will pay.
2. **Lead with the assistant that does the desk's work,** and include
   note-writing, because note-writing alone is where the free
   competition is.
3. **Test Gemini against Sarvam on real recordings before anything
   else.** If the study holds, it is both cheaper and more accurate.
4. **Measure your own funnel.** Your first 20 to 30 demos will give
   better conversion and churn numbers than anything published.
5. **Do not plan on hospitals.** Nothing found shows what they pay, and
   the assistant covers none of their modules.

## What could not be found

Number of clinics in Pune; how many clinics use any software; the share
of prescriptions still handwritten; what doctors say they would pay; how
many of Practo's doctors pay; demo-to-sale rates; sales cost; any
peer-reviewed study of an AI scribe in an Indian clinic; word-error
rates for Marathi medical speech.

Several official pages refused automated reading, so some figures were
read from search summaries. They should be opened by hand before going
into a pitch.
